 [Blackstone Edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackstone_Edge) lies on (and
in some places even forms) the boundary between Yorkshire and Greater
Manchester. I first did this walk on my own [back in February
2013](https://www.instagram.com/p/VzBFE7IjLZ). Almost 12 years later _to the
day_, and made better by having my good friend
[Ross](https://www.instagram.com/rossmthomas) with me, the weather was a bit
different this time around: overcast, a little windy, and very cold when
exposed. It was still an amazing route and a great day out, and a fun way to
spend an afternoon.

My pal Ross and I—who met because he owned one of [my favourite
bars](https://www.instagram.com/goodmoodhfx) in the North—have started going for
walks together on a weekend. This week, we headed to Blackstone Edge on the
Yorkshire–Greater Manchester border.

We met in Mytholmroyd and Ross drove us up through Cragg Vale—up [the UK’s
longest continuous
ascent](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/sport/othersports/cycling/tour-de-france/10947052/Tour-de-France-Yorkshire-villagers-break-record-for-worlds-longest-bunting.html)—to
start our journey at [Hollingworth
Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollingworth_Lake).

The route is taken directly from the AA’s [Rated
Trips](https://www.ratedtrips.com/walking/walking-ways-on-blackstone-edge). It’s
a 10km circular and is very easy to follow. The only bit of instruction you need
to be aware of is the following (emphasis mine):

> As it later swings right, take the path ahead past a picnic area, crossing
> a brook shortly afterwards to emerge on a track by Lane Foot. Go left 200yds
> (183m) and then **leave sharp right through a waymarked gate.**

While this isn’t necessarily erroneous, it is no longer correct: since the guide
was written, the owners of the adjoining farm have reworked their land boundary
a bit and thus there is no longer a gate. All other cues (a right turn and
waymarkers) remain correct, but the lack of gate did initially throw me off. The
rest of the instructions are great—incredibly clear, concise, and accurate.

The whole point of the walk, I suppose, is reaching Blackstone Edge itself via
the old (alleged) Roman road. The road has long been
thought to be a Roman-built road crossing the Pennines, but there is
considerable debate about its origins. If Roman, it would have been one of the
few trans-Pennine routes built to facilitate movement across the region. The
road would have linked to _Margary Route 712_, a known Roman road between
Manchester and Ilkley.

However, some people think otherwise—the scale and construction of the road
differ from confirmed Roman roads, and this example is unusually steep. Instead,
they believe the stone paving was added in the 18th or 19th century to improve
an already ancient route, possibly for packhorses or as part of [turnpike
road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnpike_trust) improvements.

Either way, at the top of the road sits the _Aiggin Stone_. Believed to date
back to medieval times, though some speculate it may be even older, the Aiggin
Stone is a historic waymarker that likely served as a boundary marker or
a waystone for travellers navigating the treacherous Blackstone Edge Roman Road
and moorland trails.

The view from the Aiggin Stone is expansive, but not that great on an overcast
day. If possible, try time this walk with better, clearer weather.

Once you’ve reached the Aiggin Stone, you’ve also pretty much reached the ridge,
_Blackstone Edge_. Blackstone Edge is a gritstone escarpment on the Pennine
hills, straddling the border between Rochdale, Greater Manchester, and
Calderdale, West Yorkshire. It reaches an elevation of 472 metres and it made of
coarse sandstone that formed about 320 million years ago! The trig
(triangulation) point at Blackstone Edge marks one of the highest points in the
region.

On the top, the wind picked up and the temperature plummeted, so we didn’t stay
there long. Just enough for Ross to snap a little photo of me on a rocky
outcrop.
