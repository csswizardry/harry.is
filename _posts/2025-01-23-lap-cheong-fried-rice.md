Late last year I became obsessed—I mean, _obsessed_—with fried rice. A humble
meal coupled with the utmost focus on technique make it my Asian equivalent of
an omelette—superficially a very simple dish but with plenty of scope for both
perfection or disaster depending on how you fare. This particular fried rice
has become my firm favourite. I probably make this at least once a week, with
enough for leftovers the next day.

Preparing everything in advance (your _mise en place_) is the key to any
perfectly cooked fried rice or stir fry. As you need to cook everything over
a very high heat the whole time, things come together too quickly to attempt it
any other way. Make sure you have everything to hand exactly when you require
it.

## You Will Need…

The ingredients list is minimal. You will need:

* Day-old cooked rice
  - This is non-negotiable—do not try to make this with freshly cooked rice
* Two eggs
* Three spring onions
* Lap cheong
  - Lap cheong is a cured, slightly sweet sausage from southern China
* [Salt](/cooking/with-salt/)
* [MSG](/cooking/with-salt/#bonus-round-msg--c5h8no4na)
* Light and dark soy sauce
* Sesame oil

As usual with my recipes, there is no exact science or quantities. If you end up
with leftover rice, make more fried rice; if you have leftover spring onion, put
it in a grilled cheese; if you have leftover lap cheong, snack on lap cheong.

### Day-Old Rice

If you use rice that is too fresh, it will not be dry enough. If it’s not dry
enough, it will form solid clumps in your pan as opposed to perfectly coated,
individual grains of deliciously fried rice.

Cook your rice as normal (you may have a rice cooker, but I got weirdly good at
cooking rice without one). I usually batch cook 500g at the beginning of the
week. Spread it evenly on a baking sheet to cool, and once down to room
temperature, move to the fridge loosely covered (not wrapped) with kitchen towel
or similar.

Leaving it in the fridge and barely-covered will help it dry out fully. When you
remove it from the fridge to make your fried rice, you’ll notice that parts may
have dried into solid lumps. Gently crumble these apart before cooking.

### Lap Cheong

Lap cheong is a <q>dried, hard sausage usually made from pork and pork fat. It
is normally smoked, sweetened, and seasoned with rose water, rice wine and soy
sauce</q>. – [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_sausage)

## Let’s Go!

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-mise-en-place.jpg" alt="Mise en place for lap cheong fried rice with diced sausage, chopped spring onions, and whisked eggs." width="1280" height="720">
  <figcaption>Mise en place is vital for any fast-moving dish.</figcaption>
</figure>

Prepare your ingredients by beating two eggs together, slicing and separating
the whites and greens of three spring onions, and dicing three lap cheong
sausages (or six mini ones).

That’s all the ingredients done! Now it’s time to cook.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-oil.jpg" alt="Hot oil in a wok, ready for cooking lap cheong fried rice." width="1280" height="720" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Begin by heating a neutral **oil** in a high-sided pan or wok. I tend to use
canola oil for most day-to-day cooking, which might surprise you, but I’ll write
about that one another time.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-egg.jpg" alt="Egg mixture in a wok, being stirred with chopsticks as part of the fried rice preparation." width="960" height="1280" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Add your **eggs**, lightly season with salt, and begin to scramble them until
80–90% cooked.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-egg-cooked.jpg" alt="Eggs scrambled separately and ready to be added to lap cheong fried rice." width="960" height="1280" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Remove the partially cooked eggs and set aside.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-sausage-cooking.jpg" alt="Chinese sausage (lap cheong) being stir-fried in a wok to release its oils and aroma for fried rice." width="1280" height="720" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Wipe the pan clean and add a drizzle more oil if necessary. The sausage will
release some fat of its own so don’t add too much extra. Slowly begin to render
out the **sausage** over the same high heat.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-sausage-onion.jpg" alt="Lap cheong sausage and spring onions stir-frying together in a wok, forming the base for the fried rice." width="1280" height="720" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Once visibly beginning to cook, add the whites of the **spring onion** and
season lightly with salt. Cook these together for a few minutes until the onions
have started to soften.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-rice.jpg" alt="Cooked jasmine rice being stir-fried with lap cheong in a wok." width="960" height="1280" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Once the onions are starting to colour (you can just see one that’s clung to the
side of the pan above), add a couple of handfuls of **rice**. For reference,
I’ve added about 250g of rice _before cooking_ to this pan.

Add a good dash more oil to coat the rice and salt again. It’s important to
keep everything moving from here on in. Toss and stir and toss and stir,
constantly.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-soy.jpg" alt="Fried rice with lap cheong, soy sauce, and spring onions in a wok nearing completion." width="960" height="1280" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Now it’s time to season! Pour a generous amount of **light soy** sauce around
the edge of the pan so that it runs down and into the rice. Light soy will add
flavour. Then, in the same way, add a little less **dark soy**. Dark soy will
add flavour, naturally, but also beautiful colour. Taste and adjust, but
I generally tend to find that 2:1 light to dark soy seems to work best.

If your soy doesn’t have any added **MSG**, now would be a good time to add
a sprinkle yourself.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-egg-added.jpg" alt="Freshly cooked fried rice with lap cheong and egg, in a wok during the cooking process." width="960" height="1280" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Now, and you can barely see it in the photo above (it was difficult to take
photos while cooking a fried rice), it’s time to introduce your **egg**. As
before, keep things moving—toss the pan to ensure the egg cooks the rest of the
way through and gets evenly distributed among the rice.

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-onion.jpg" alt="Spring onion greens added to colour and garnish lap cheong fried rice." width="960" height="1280" loading="lazy">
</figure>

We’re 90% there! For your final move, kill the heat (or remove the pan) and add
in the **greens** of the onions. Toss to distribute and adjust for seasoning one
final time. That whole process should have only taken a few minutes!

Dish the rice into a warmed bowl and drizzle with a small amount of **sesame
oil** to taste. This will add a subtle but unmistakable finish that should not
be overlooked!

<figure>
  <img src="/img/content/2025/01/lap-cheong-aerial.jpg" alt="Aerial view of a bowl of lap cheong fried rice, garnished with spring onions and scrambled eggs." width="720" height="720" loading="lazy">
</figure>

Any leftover fried rice will taste amazing cold for lunch the next day. I’d
recommend letting it cool before adding to a container, drizzling with a little
more dark soy and little more sesame oil, giving it a good shake, and putting it
in the fridge for tomorrow.

This one is highly addictive. Enjoy.
